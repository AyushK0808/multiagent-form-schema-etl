% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
%  ABSTRACT
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
We study a schema-aware information extraction pipeline for semi-structured documents that reconciles two extractors field by field: an OCR-free Donut branch and an OCR-LLM branch (a text LLM over OCR text with a vision-language fallback). A deterministic, evidence-based fusion policy chooses between them and records why each value was chosen, and a schema registry retrieves field schemas by embedding similarity. Separately, we train LoRA adapters for LayoutLMv3 layout labelling, which reach a test macro-F1 of 0.774 (receipts and invoices) and 0.739 (forms and multi-domain pages), mean over three seeds, while training about 1\% of the parameters, against 0.706 and 0.550 for a frozen backbone; these adapters are evaluated on their own and are not part of the field-extraction path. We evaluate the pipeline on SROIE (100 receipts), CORD (50 receipts) and a 15-document synthetic set, and compare it with a local 7B vision-language model (Qwen2.5-VL) that reads the page image and the schema directly. The fusion matches its OCR-LLM branch (token-overlap F1 0.801 against 0.816 on SROIE, 0.836 against 0.818 on CORD) and removes Donut answers that the page text does not support, the one fusion component with a measurable effect (+0.031 and +0.011 F1). It does not beat the vision-language model alone (0.992 on SROIE, 0.843 on CORD), and it is not faster. We therefore report the pipeline as an auditable, modular alternative and a negative result on fusion for these benchmarks, not as an accuracy improvement.
\end{abstract}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
%  I. INTRODUCTION
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}

The increasing digital adoption of business processes generates vast amounts of semi-structured documents that need to be effectively analyzed. These documents typically contain natural language text as well as non-textual elements such as layout and column structure; hence, the position of specific fields within these documents can vary drastically between different versions, vendors, and countries. Consequently, attempting to extract information based on the position of such fields will not be viable.

Historically, process and rules-based methods were employed to extract the information from these documents. Such methods were replaced by layout-aware models like LayoutLM \cite{layoutlm}, LayoutLMv2 \cite{layoutlmv2}, and LayoutLMv3 \cite{layoutlmv3}; more recently, models like Donut \cite{kim2022donut} that do not require optical character recognition and are agnostic to the layout of the document have been introduced.

Furthermore, with the introduction of large language models (LLMs) \cite{gptner2024}, it is possible to automatically extract information from documents in a largely unsupervised manner. However, LLMs tend to generate unanchored outputs when extracting information from documents \cite{ji2023hallucination}. One way to address this problem is to incorporate machine learning models that utilize deterministic verification mechanisms. We propose a framework that simultaneously utilizes layout-aware and OCR-free models, as well as a field-level deterministic fusion module that utilizes information regarding each field’s availability, agreement, confidence, attributes, and dependencies to deterministically fuse field candidates.

In addition to the two extraction branches, we incorporate schema retrieval and train parameter-efficient (LoRA) adapters for LayoutLMv3 layout labelling. The adapters are evaluated on their own; the current field-extraction path does not consume them (Section~\ref{sec:arch}).

Our goal is not to claim the best way to extract information from documents. We build the pipeline, measure each component against a strong baseline (a local vision-language model that reads the page image and the schema directly), and report where the components help and where they do not. The main findings are: (i) LoRA adapters give a clear gain over a frozen backbone at about 1\% trainable parameters; (ii) grounding Donut answers in the OCR text is the one fusion component with a measurable benefit; (iii) on receipts, fusing the two specialist branches does not beat a 7B vision-language model alone, and does not reduce latency; and (iv) the fusion layer keeps a per-field record of which branch supplied each value and why.

%  II. RELATED WORK
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Layout-aware models.} LayoutLM\cite{layoutlm}, LayoutLMv2\cite{layoutlmv2}, LayoutLMv3\cite{layoutlmv3} and GeoLayoutLM\cite{luo2023geolayoutlm} pre-train on text, position and image features and are fine-tuned per task on datasets such as FUNSD\cite{jaume2019funsd}, CORD\cite{park2019cord} and SROIE\cite{huang2019sroie}; FormNet\cite{lee2022formnet} and Hwang et al.\cite{hwang2021cost} model form label--value structure explicitly. Layout-labelling corpora such as PubLayNet\cite{publaynet}, DocBank\cite{docbank} and DocLayNet\cite{pfitzmann2022doclaynet} supply region labels, and Binmakhashen and Mahmoud\cite{binmakhashen2020} survey the task. These models need a task-specific head and labelled data per schema; we adapt LayoutLMv3 with LoRA to layout labels over four datasets and report it as a layout labeller. \emph{Gap:} none of this work reconciles a layout model with other extractors under a schema given at run time.

\textbf{OCR-free models.} Donut\cite{kim2022donut} and Dessurt\cite{davis2022dessurt} generate structured output directly from the page image. We use the public Donut checkpoint fine-tuned on DocVQA\cite{mathew2021docvqa}, which we pose one question per field, and find that its token probabilities carry no usable information about correctness. \emph{Gap:} the output must be checked against the page text before it can be trusted next to another extractor.

\textbf{LLM and vision-language extraction.} Prompting methods such as GPT-NER\cite{gptner2024} extract information with little annotation, but language models can produce unsupported outputs\cite{ji2023hallucination}, and long contracts, for which datasets such as CUAD\cite{cuad2021} provide clause-level labels, remain hard. Iterative agent methods\cite{react2023,reflexion2023,selfrefine2023} let a model revise its own output; our fusion is a single deterministic pass instead. Vision-language models such as Qwen2.5-VL\cite{bai2025qwen25vl} read the page image directly and follow a requested output schema, and we use a locally served 7B model both as a baseline and as the vision fallback of the OCR-LLM branch. \emph{Gap:} it is unclear whether specialist branches plus fusion add anything over such a model, which is the question our evaluation addresses.

\textbf{Parameter-efficient adaptation.} LoRA\cite{hu2022lora} adapts a model by training low-rank updates, and He et al.\cite{he2022unified} relate adapters, prefix tuning and LoRA in one framework. Application to layout-aware document models across several document styles is less studied; we train one adapter per document style (receipts and invoices, forms and multi-domain pages) and evaluate on held-out test splits and across groups. Graph-based key-information methods such as PICK\cite{yu2021pick} are related work for modelling spatial structure, which our pipeline uses only through OCR bounding boxes.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:gap}

\subsection{Research Gap}

Current document information extraction methods mostly focus on either single layout-aware or OCR-free models, and evaluate them against schema-specific tasks. However, deterministic, auditable reconciliation of an OCR-free generator with an OCR-text extractor, inside a pipeline that also covers schema retrieval and validation, has yet to be compared with a plain vision-language model baseline. 



\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Given a semi-structured PDF document, and a schema of typed fields along with their description and constraints, we must output field-value pairs which must satisfy the given constraints, are traceable to the original document, are reconciled by a deterministic rule set, and have an associated confidence score in [0, 1]. The document may be native or scanned(with OCR on it), and the schema can either be pre-defined or retrieved on the fly from a schema registry.

\section{System Architecture and Methodology}
\label{sec:arch}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Pipeline architecture. The LoRA layout adapters (dashed) are evaluated on their own and are not used by the extraction branches.}
    \label{fig:system_architecture}
\end{figure}

This pipeline has eight stages of extraction. Each stage passes a shared state from previous stage(s) to next (including any error it encounters), consisting of the blocks of documents being ingested, the image of the rendered page, the adapters that have been chosen for use, the results from the two extractors that were run to produce an extraction bundle, the fusion of policies applied to the output of the two extractors, the final resolved schema after all of the above steps are done, validation outcomes for each field after validation was run, a reference key for storing the data to be stored and any accumulated errors. An adapter group is selected once from the identified or retrieved schema name; in the evaluated build the selected adapter is carried in the state but is not consumed by either extraction branch, so the end-to-end results below do not measure the adapters. Each stage will process exceptions independently. So if there is a problem in one stage then subsequent stages can continue with a partial state instead of failing completely.

\subsection{Document Ingestion with OCR Fallback}

The ingestion stage accepts PDF files as input, and produces a stream of document blocks. Each document block contains information about a single page in the PDF file, including a page number, all text that was found on the page, a bounding rectangle defined by four points $(x_0,y_0,x_1,y_1)$, a confidence value and a source tag describing how the text was obtained (either "native" or "ocr"). For digital PDFs, we attempt to get native text through PyMuPDF first. We consider native extraction insufficient for pages that contain less than 20 characters or five words. Pages that do not meet these criteria are rendered into images at a doubled horizontal and vertical scale factor ($2\times$). However, the scale factor is halved if $\max(\text{width},\text{height})>5000$ pixels in order to limit both memory usage and processing cost. In addition to other information contained within the OCR derived block, each block also includes an individual word level extraction confidence that is provided by Tesseract. This confidence is used later down-stream by the policy fusion layer.

\subsection{LoRA Adapter-Routed Layout Analysis}

\subsubsection{Dataset Grouping}

Four datasets that carry token- or region-level layout annotations are split into two groups by document style: Group~1 (receipts and invoices: CORD and SROIE) and Group~2 (forms and multi-domain pages: FUNSD and DocLayNet, capped at 5{,}000 training pages). The two groups are kept separate because their layout statistics differ, so a single adapter would have to trade one against the other; one adapter per group also lets the router load only what a document needs. Group~1 and Group~2 are fine-tuned independently on top of a frozen LayoutLMv3 base model, each with its own LoRA adapter (training one group does not depend on the other). The datasets without such labels (e.g., DocVQA, Kleister-NDA) are not used for adapter training. The CORD and SROIE training splits are part of Group~1's training data; the adapter only predicts layout classes, never field values, so this does not leak extraction answers into the end-to-end benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:eval}, but we state it for completeness.

\subsubsection{Adapter Setup}

A LoRA adapter for all Query, Key, Value, and Output projection layers in the LayoutLMv3 encoder are trained, with hyperparameter settings in Table \ref{tab:hyperparams}. A single adapter-router can maintain the frozen base model and keep a few recently-used adapters in memory, and can switch between groups of adapters by using PEFT commands \texttt{set\adapter} and \texttt{load\adapter}.

\subsubsection{Unifying Heterogeneous Label Spaces}

 The four datasets have different native label sets and levels of detail (word-level entities in FUNSD, region categories in DocLayNet, receipt fields in CORD and SROIE). We map all of them onto one shared set of six layout labels: paragraph, heading, list item, table, caption, and other. Both adapters use this same set, which keeps them comparable and allows cross-group evaluation. Token-level labeling is achieved by including token centers in segments, instead of exact positional matching. For each OCR token (which consists of the OCR text and a bounding box normalized coordinates into [0,1000] and represented as $w = (x0^w, y0^w, x1^w, y1^w)$, we check for inclusion of the token's center point into annotated segments and assign the first containing segment as the token label, or use a background label if it is not inside any. This process can unify different supervisions in word-, region-, and document-level data.

\subsection{Parallel Extraction}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{parallel_ex.pdf}
    \caption{Parallel extraction, grounding of Donut answers and policy fusion.}
    \label{fig:parallel_extraction}
\end{figure}

We run the Donut and OCR-LLM extractors on 2 worker threads and output a bundle which includes, for each extractor, a map of field predictions, a confidence map and relevant spatial information.

\subsubsection{Donut OCR-Free Extraction}

 Each field of a schema $f_i$ is extracted by posing a question, composed based on the schema description (like ``What is the invoice date?''), to Donut with the document image. The output consists of:
\begin{equation}
\text{answer}_i = \text{Donut}(\text{image}, q_i)
\end{equation}
The confidence for field $i$ is computed as the average maximum softmax
probability across generated tokens:
\begin{equation}
    c_i^{\text{donut}} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \max_{v} \text{softmax}(s_t)_v
    \label{eq:donut_conf}
\end{equation}
where $s_t$ is the logit vector at generation step $t$ and $T$ is the total
number of generated tokens. Donut's weights are loaded lazily on first use, so
pipelines that never invoke the Donut branch incur no initialization cost.

\subsubsection{Two-Stage FormFiller with Null Handling}

The OCR-LLM extraction is based on a two-step procedure. (The branch was called the ``LayoutLM branch'' in the first version of this paper and in our code; it does not run LayoutLMv3. Spatial information enters only through the bounding boxes of the OCR blocks to which an extracted value is matched, and the LoRA adapters of the previous subsection are not used here.) First, a text LLM is given the first 4000 characters of the document together with all field descriptions, resulting in an initial JSON extraction. Second, for any remaining empty field from step 1, we input the field together with the document image to a vision language model in order to infer image- grounded values such as those from a stamp, a table or a signature.

\subsection{Reflexive Policy Fusion}

The policy fusion layer combines both extractor outputs with deterministic rules and keeps a record, per field, of the branch that supplied the value and the reason. Donut's token probabilities are uncalibrated in practice (we observed 0.9--1.0 whether the answer was right or wrong), so confidence is never the deciding signal between the two branches; it is used only as a floor below which a lone value is discarded. The OCR-LLM value is the default, and Donut takes over only on evidence that the OCR-LLM value is unusable while its own value is usable. A value is judged unusable if it does not parse as the field's type (a date without a four-digit year, a non-numeric amount), if the same value was given to several fields, or if it is a clause of more than ten words rather than a value. Fields with a strong spatial anchor (dates, amounts, signatures) always take the OCR-LLM value on conflict. Before fusion, each Donut answer is grounded: an answer that appears in no OCR block of the page is dropped, and a supported answer takes the confidence of the block it was found in. A Donut value that fills a field the OCR-LLM branch left empty is accepted for non-numeric fields only (the \texttt{non\_numeric} setting), because Donut-only numeric fills were the main source of wrong values in the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:eval}.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:fusion} summarizes the procedure for a single field.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} OCR-LLM value $v_O$, confidence $c_O$; grounded Donut value $v_D$, confidence $c_D$; confidence floor $\delta$; field type $\tau$
\IF{$v_O$ and $v_D$ are both null}
    \STATE \textbf{return} null
\ELSIF{$v_D$ is null}
    \STATE \textbf{return} $v_O$ if $c_L \geq \delta$, else null
\ELSIF{$v_O$ is null}
    \STATE \textbf{return} $v_D$ if $c_D \geq \delta$, $v_D$ is usable, and $\tau$ is not numeric; else null
\ELSIF{canonical($v_O$) $=$ canonical($v_D$)}
    \STATE \textbf{return} $v_O$ \COMMENT{agreement; OCR-LLM value retained}
\ELSIF{field is spatially anchored}
    \STATE \textbf{return} $v_O$
\ELSIF{$v_O$ is unusable \AND $v_D$ is usable}
    \STATE \textbf{return} $v_D$ \COMMENT{evidence, not confidence}
\ELSE
    \STATE \textbf{return} $v_O$ \COMMENT{default}
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\begin{center}
\textit{Algorithm 1: Deterministic field-level policy fusion.}
\end{center}
\label{alg:fusion}

During fusion, normalization is performed, too: a standard technique to filter out common legal jargon, like "as defined herein", removing white spaces and punctuation.

Finally, a cross-field check is done which ensures that dates are consistent (effectivedate $\le$ expirydate), that disclosure and receiving parties are not the same, etc., and marks failed fields.
($\text{effective\_date} \leq \text{expiry\_date}$) and party uniqueness
($\text{disclosing\_party} \neq \text{receiving\_party}$); fields that fail
these checks are flagged with a warning that is propagated into record
metadata rather than being silently corrected.

\subsection{Schema Management, Validation, and Storage}

 The schemas are stored as averaged embeddings (via all-MiniLM-L6-v2) of the field descriptions, and they are fetched using cosine similarity. Schema matching, field normalization, new schema generation in case of a poor match are done by Llama-3.3-70b. The process of record extraction is just checking if the required fields satisfy the type and value constraints.
Invalid fields can be retried or assigned the default value defined by the schema.
All valid records are saved to a SQLITE database.

\section{Experimental Results and Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The LoRA adapters were trained on a Windows~11 laptop with Python~3.12. We used AdamW as the optimizer with a mini-batch size of 2 and accumulated gradients over 8 steps (total effective batch size of 16). We used bf16 mixed-precision training on a single NVIDIA RTX~5060 Laptop GPU (8\,GB) and trained up to 10 epochs with early stopping on validation macro-F1 score with a patience of 3 epochs. No data augmentation was used. Each configuration was trained with three seeds (13, 42, 1234); the train/validation split is fixed across seeds, and the official test splits of the datasets are used only for the final evaluation. Validation sets are the own validation splits of the datasets where they exist (CORD, DocLayNet) and a seeded 10\% hold-out of the training split otherwise (SROIE, FUNSD). The full configuration is listed in Table~\ref{tab:env}; every run writes its hyperparameters (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}) to a JSON file.

We evaluate the complete pipeline and its branches on three test sets: a seeded random subset of 100 SROIE receipts (four fields: company, date, address, total), a seeded subset of 50 CORD receipts (six amount fields), and the 15-document synthetic set used in the earlier version of this work (invoices, NDAs, employment contracts, land deeds and tender instruments, 30 fields). The subsets are drawn once with a fixed seed from the official test splits, and no setting was tuned on the SROIE or CORD test subsets. F1 is the field-level token-overlap F1 after normalization (partial credit), and EM is the share of gold values matched exactly after normalization. All model stages run locally: Donut-base fine-tuned on DocVQA, Llama~3.2~3B for the OCR-LLM branch's text pass, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B served by Ollama (temperature 0, 8192-token context) for the vision fallback and for the VLM baseline. The schema-agent repair step of the full system uses a hosted small model. Each run was executed once; we did not measure run-to-run variation.

Table~\ref{tab:e2e_aggregate} gives the main result, and it is mixed. The full pipeline is within 0.02 F1 of its OCR-LLM branch on every set (below it on SROIE, above it on CORD, equal on the synthetic set), so the fusion does not add accuracy on top of the better of its two specialist branches. A 7B vision-language model that reads the page and the schema directly is therefore the strongest single extractor in our setting, and the specialist stack does not close the gap. The synthetic set is close to ceiling for every strong system, so it cannot separate components and is reported as a sanity check.

\ without value normalization              & 0.799 & 0.836 \\
\ without spatial-field rule               & 0.799 & 0.836 \\
\ Donut fills all empty fields             & 0.801 & 0.810 \\
\ Donut fills no empty fields              & 0.801 & 0.836 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that only one component has a measurable effect. Grounding Donut answers in the OCR text adds 0.031 F1 on SROIE and 0.011 on CORD. Letting Donut fill every empty field, numeric ones included, lowers CORD F1 from 0.836 to 0.810 and raises the hallucination rate from 0.148 to 0.192, which is why the default accepts Donut-only values for non-numeric fields only; this default was fixed before the test runs, and on these sets it coincides with disabling Donut fills altogether. The schema-agent repair, normalization and spatial rule each change F1 by at most 0.002 and cannot be distinguished from noise at these sample sizes.

\subsubsection{Failure Analysis}

On SROIE the gap to the VLM comes from the free-text and amount fields. Per-field F1 for the full pipeline against the VLM is 0.76 against 0.99 for address, 0.77 against 0.99 for company and 0.76 against 1.00 for total, while date is close (0.92 against 0.99, with an exact-match rate of 0.57 for every system, a date-format effect). The OCR-LLM branch is the default in the fusion policy, so its errors on these fields carry through: the evidence rules override it only when its value is unparseable, duplicated across fields or clause-like, and a wrong but well-formed address or total is none of these. On CORD the pipeline and the VLM differ on one field only, credit-card amount (0.89 against 1.00, eight receipts). Both share the hardest fields, tax (0.74) and subtotal (0.78).

The hallucination rates on CORD (0.148 for the pipeline, 0.151 for the OCR-LLM branch, 0.135 for the VLM) are dominated by one field. Of the 26 predictions of the VLM that fall where the gold value is empty, 18 are subtotals and 16 of those copy another gold field of the same receipt, almost always the total: the receipt prints no subtotal line, and the model fills the field from the total. We report the rate as measured; excluding the subtotal field it falls to 5.6\% for the VLM. On CORD, EM equals recall for every system because every field is a single numeric token, so token overlap scores each field 0 or 1.

CORD also exposes the OCR dependence of the pipeline. Tesseract returns only about 10 text blocks per receipt (5 of the 56 cached receipts have none), so grounding discards almost every Donut answer, and the Donut-only and text-pass rows of Table~\ref{tab:e2e_aggregate} are limited by OCR and are not a verdict on those models in isolation. Donut itself produced about six non-empty fields per receipt before grounding. The VLM, which reads the image, is not affected.

\subsubsection{Latency}

We timed 15 documents (5 per dataset, one warm-up document discarded) on the laptop used for training, with response caching disabled. The Qwen2.5-VL-7B model runs partly from system memory (about 14\% CPU, 86\% GPU) on the 8\,GB card. Median per-stage times are: OCR 1.2\,s; Donut 8.5\,s (6.6\,s on SROIE, 8.5\,s on CORD, 17\,s on the multi-page synthetic documents); OCR-LLM branch 19.9\,s; fusion under 10\,ms. The OCR-LLM branch takes about 6\,s when the text pass fills every field and about 20\,s when it calls the vision model for the remainder. A standalone VLM call takes about 20\,s per page (15.8--22.3\,s on SROIE, 19.5--20.6\,s on the synthetic set). The full pipeline without a separate VLM branch has a median of 29.0\,s per document, against about 20\,s for the VLM alone. One receipt took 126.8\,s in the OCR-LLM branch because the model looped and was reloaded. Two measurement caveats apply: when the OCR-LLM branch had just issued the same request, Ollama answered the follow-up VLM call from its prompt cache in about 2\,s, and we do not report those rows as VLM latency; and with 15 documents the means are sensitive to outliers, so we give medians. The pipeline is thus not faster than the VLM, and its cost is dominated by Donut and by the OCR-LLM branch's own use of the VLM.

\subsubsection{Limitations of the Evaluation}

The LoRA adapters are not part of the evaluated field-extraction path, so the end-to-end results say nothing about their value for field extraction; wiring them in (for example as region features in the OCR-LLM prompt) is future work. The benchmark subsets are small (100 and 50 receipts), and differences of under 0.02 F1 between the pipeline and its OCR-LLM branch should not be read as significant. Each configuration was run once. The synthetic set was written by the authors, and the fusion rules were developed while looking at its results and at CORD validation outputs, so it is not an unbiased test. The two public sets are receipts with few fields, so they do not exercise the clause-level and multi-page cases that motivated the Donut branch; behaviour on long contracts is evaluated only on the 15 synthetic documents. Finally, Qwen2.5-VL's training data may include receipt corpora, so the VLM's advantage on SROIE and CORD may overstate its advantage on private document types.

\subsection{Adapter Storage Efficiency}

Each LoRA adapter has 1.18M trainable parameters (0.94\% of 126.5M) and its weights occupy 4.5\,MB, compared to roughly 500\,MB for a full LayoutLMv3 checkpoint. Training took about 260\,s per epoch for Group~1 (1{,}363 pages) and about 1{,}000\,s per epoch for Group~2 (4{,}954 pages) on the 8\,GB laptop GPU, with a peak GPU memory of 2.9\,GB. These are single-machine measurements taken while other jobs shared the GPU, so they are indicative rather than exact.

\subsection{Schema Registry Retrieval}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{schema_registry}
    \caption{Precision--recall trade-off for schema registry similarity search
    at differing cosine similarity thresholds}
    \label{fig:schema_pr_curve}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:schema_pr_curve} shows the retrieval trade-off as the cosine similarity threshold $\sigma$ varies from 0.30 to 0.95. The registry holds eight schemas (four document types from the stress set, SROIE and CORD receipts, purchase orders, and forms). The query set contains 50 labelled field-name sets: 40 belong to a registered schema (exact names, synonym renames, subsets, and subsets with unrelated extra fields; five per schema) and 10 describe unregistered document types and must be rejected. A query is answered with its top-1 schema if the similarity reaches $\sigma$. At the operating threshold $\sigma=0.70$ used throughout this paper, precision is 1.00 and no unregistered query is accepted, but recall is only 0.575, so a large share of renamed or partial field sets fall through to the schema agent. Lowering the threshold raises recall: at $\sigma=0.45$ precision is 0.889, recall 0.800, and still no unregistered query is accepted; at $\sigma=0.40$ precision and recall are both 0.825 but 30\% of unregistered queries are wrongly accepted. We keep $\sigma=0.70$ as the conservative default because a wrong schema match is costlier than a fallback; since this query set was written by the authors and is small, the curve indicates the trade-off rather than an optimised threshold.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
%  VI. CONCLUSION
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper builds a schema-aware extraction pipeline from an OCR-free Donut branch, an OCR-LLM branch, a deterministic evidence-based fusion policy and a schema registry, and measures it against a local vision-language model. Separately, LoRA adapters for LayoutLMv3 layout labelling reach a test macro-F1 of 0.774 and 0.739 (mean over three seeds) while training 0.94\% of the parameters, against 0.706 and 0.550 for a frozen backbone, and each adapter occupies 4.5\,MB; they are not yet used by the extraction branches. End to end, the fusion matches its OCR-LLM branch (F1 0.801 against 0.816 on SROIE, 0.836 against 0.818 on CORD), and grounding Donut answers in the OCR text is the one component with a measurable benefit (+0.031 and +0.011 F1). The fusion does not outperform Qwen2.5-VL-7B, which reaches 0.992 on SROIE and 0.843 on CORD, and the pipeline's median latency (29\,s per document) is not lower than the VLM's (about 20\,s per page). On these receipt benchmarks a single local vision-language model is the better extractor, and we do not claim an accuracy advantage for the specialist stack. What the pipeline offers is modularity and a per-field record of the source and reason for each chosen value, which a single model call does not give. The evaluation is limited by small subsets, single runs, sparse OCR on CORD and receipt-style documents with few fields; the next step is to test long, multi-page contracts, where Donut may matter more, with repeated runs, and to connect the layout adapters to field extraction.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
%  VII. FUTURE WORK
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Future directions include using the LoRA layout adapters inside field extraction; fusing the vision-language model's answers into the policy as a third source, using evidence rules developed on validation splits only; replacing Tesseract with a stronger OCR engine so that grounding does not discard Donut's answers on small receipts; evaluating on long multi-page contracts with public annotations; broadening the capabilities to multilingual documents by augmenting with other adapters trained over resources such as XFUND and CORD \cite{park2019cord}; and introducing a graph-based table detector \cite{yu2021pick} for table content extraction.
